Behaviour analysis

Produced with scripts/analyze.py (raw numbers in runs/analysis/*.json). Unless stated, figures are for the evolution champion g3m05 over 100 levels x 5 episodes, 1 life, one-minute cap, sampled actions.

The headline: the agent is a turret

The multi-level agents never move sideways. The player starts at the bottom-left (x = 8) and the agent stays within a few pixels of that spot for the whole episode, in cleared episodes too. It faces the left wall 97-99% of the time, so its bubbles form right where it stands and ride the air currents up the left edge. It jumps (the champion stays between y = 27 and 52) and shoots, and otherwise waits for enemies to come to it.

  • The champion's gain came from selection, not the learning rate. The control, which is exactly the champion's recipe minus the selection step, scores the same as the baseline (211 vs 217). The evolutionary loop earned its +85.
  • Random starts did not teach movement. Trained from random platforms, the agent still walks to a wall and camps; from the normal start it is worse than before (167), and under random starts it clears 44 / 300 against 45 for the control, so it gained nothing from the training condition either.
  • A moving agent does not become a good one in 30M steps.   • Camping is an attractor. Given 100M steps instead of 30M, the moving specialist did not get better at moving: it stopped moving. By the end it stands at the spawn point facing the wall 99% of the time, exactly like the agents trained from scratch, and is worse than the baseline because it camps less effectively.
  • Forcing movement from the start gives an agent that moves and dies. Trained from random platforms from scratch, the agent crosses the screen but dies in 92% of episodes from the normal start.

So within this network and training setup, every road leads to the corner. The likeliest reason it is such a good corner: six of the eight enemy kinds are stand-ins without their projectiles (game.py). In the real game a Stoner's rocks, a Hidegonsu's fireballs or an Invader's lasers would punish a stationary player; here nothing does, so standing in a spot that enemies walk into while bubbles rise around you is close to optimal. Implementing the projectile enemies is probably the single change most likely to produce an agent that plays like a person, and it is a fidelity fix rather than an RL trick.

A different network does not change the picture either. --arch conv (a small CNN over the 16 x 24 grid plus an MLP over the rest, about 500k parameters, from scratch, 50M steps):

Every agent that scores well stands in the bottom-left corner. The fix is most likely in the game, not the learner: give the six stand-in enemy kinds their projectiles so that standing still is punished the way it is in the original.

After implementing the projectiles (2026-10-06 evening)

All eight enemy kinds now follow bub_ai.z80: Stoners throw rocks that roll until they hit a wall, Hidegonsus shoot fireballs that pass through walls, Invaders drop lasers on a timer, Drunks throw a boomerang beer and freeze until it returns, Hullaballoons float faster than Belugas, Banebous bounce. Every projectile kills the player; bubbles do not stop them.

Projectiles lowered the ceiling of camping (115 instead of 217) but did not make movement learnable: the agents that camp still beat the agents that move by a wide margin, and a moving initialisation collapses into camping again. So the fidelity fix was necessary but not sufficient. The remaining suspect is the representation: in the "state" observation every object is an absolute (x, y) in a fixed slot and the layout is 384 unrelated numbers, so "a platform two tiles above me with an enemy walking toward me" looks completely different on every level. A person sees that situation the same way everywhere.

Where this leaves things (2026-10-07)

Across two days: about 1.5 billion agent steps, 7 ideas, 3 observation designs, 2 architectures. The reimplementation now has all eight enemy kinds with projectiles, and the camping exploit is weaker than it was, but no training recipe tried here learns to move and survive across 100 layouts. The best scores in the current game are still campers (115 / 1000).

What has not been tried, roughly in order of how likely it is to work:

  1. Learning from Scott's own play. Record button presses while playing (play.py already has the loop), then pretrain the policy by behaviour cloning before PPO. Human play is moving play by construction, and PPO fine-tuning from a moving policy is a different starting point from the ones that collapsed here, because the collapse happened when the policy had never been rewarded for moving. A few hundred human episodes is enough for a first attempt.
  2. Memory. The policy has none: with sampled actions and no recurrence it cannot commit to "go right until the gap", which is what moving across a level requires. A GRU over the ego observation is the standard fix.
  3. A much bigger budget. Atari-style agents use hundreds of millions to billions of frames with much larger networks; these runs used 4-16M frames per level-equivalent.
  4. Fewer levels. An agent for 10 levels would show whether the method works at all before scaling to 100.

Punishing time and standing still (2026-10-08/09)

Scott asked for a heavier time penalty, a light penalty for standing still, and to keep the bubble-climbing (jumping up a wall on a column of bubbles) that the agents had started to show, as something they do in bursts rather than all the time. stationary_penalty was added to the env: a per-step charge whenever the player's x has stayed inside a band for a window of steps. Four rounds, all in the game with projectiles, scored on the usual fresh seeds (100 levels x 10 episodes) with scripts/analyze.py's new behaviour profile (3 episodes per level). What happened, round by round:

  1. The heavier time penalty brought the climbing. Bubble bounces went from 3 a minute (the old camper) to 20-28 in every run, and the fine-tuned camper became the best agent in the game. The stationary penalty did make agents move, crossing the screen in half to two-thirds of episodes, but they died within seconds: median death at 4 s, 60% of actions RIGHT. With per-step charges of 0.015 and a death penalty of 1, a minute alive costs 13.5 and dying costs 1, so running into the first enemy was the optimal policy. A reward bug, not a behaviour.
  2. Death penalty 15 fixed the suicide and killed the climbing. Bounces dropped to zero; with death that expensive a jump is a risk not worth taking. And a new loophole: agents travelled 350-650 px per episode while staying within 7 px, wiggling in place to dodge the "still" charge.
  3. Death penalty 5 restored the climbing (bouncer: 26.5 bounces/min, 126 cleared) but every run, including the former mover, settled into wiggling.
  4. A band wiggling cannot satisfy (32 px of range over 8 s) ended the wiggling, and the agents simply paid the penalty instead: 91-94% of steps still, x range 3-6 px. The bouncer improved again (141 cleared, 27 bounces/min, 46 px of vertical range); the mover gave up moving and jumping both.

So the two asks pull against each other at these strengths. A stationary penalty light enough to leave the climbing alone is also light enough to ignore; one heavy enough to force movement produces running and dying, because a moving policy that survives has still not been learned by any method tried here. The time penalty, on the other hand, did exactly what was hoped: the best agent now climbs its bubble columns constantly (gallery: exports/v6_bouncer_band32/index.html), and it is the strongest agent in the current game at 141 / 1000.

Not tried: a stationary penalty in the 0.02-0.05 range with death penalty 5 (would force movement, probably at the cost of the climbing and of clears); rewarding distance covered rather than charging for its absence; and the imitation route from the previous section, which remains the most likely way to get a moving agent that survives.

What the ten generations found:

  • No child beat the baseline in generation 1, and the unmutated continuation scored 45 against the baseline's 70. Resuming a trained agent with a fresh optimiser at the default learning rate (2.5e-4) damages it for the first several million steps. Every eventual survivor had a learning rate of 1e-4 or lower (the final ten: 3e-5 to 6e-5), and "lr down" was the only mutation that was neutral on average (-2.9 against -4.7 for leaving the genome alone). The search mostly discovered the right fine-tuning learning rate.
  • Everything else hurt, averaged over all children against their parents on the same seeds: both directions of time penalty, death penalty, capture bonus, clip and gamma lost 6-14 episodes; turning the pop reward off lost 28. The reward recipe the run started from is a local optimum for this agent.
  • Heavier stationary penalties did not make it move. Hand-picked variants with still-penalty 0.02 and 0.05 scored 55-67 of 500 in generation 1 and were not kept; the one line with 0.02 that survived a while (death penalty 10) was gone by generation 4. Nothing in the final ten strays far from the base recipe except the learning rate and a slightly higher gamma (0.993-0.995).
  • Gains flattened after generation 3: best-of-generation sat at 82-90 for the remaining seven, with the mean of the population creeping up as the low-learning-rate lines took over.

How to look at it: uv run mlflow ui --backend-store-uri sqlite:///mlflow.db, experiment evolve-evo10. Sort by eval_cleared, filter tags.survived = "True", or open a child to see its policy loss, entropy and value loss over its 8M steps next to its evaluation.

Training setup and results

The best model so far (runs/ppo_state_all_50M/model.pt) was trained with scripts/train_ppo.py: PPO, the structured state observation (not pixels), a random level from all 100 each episode (--levels 1-100), time penalty 0.002, 50M agent steps. Earlier models trained on level 1 only for 2M steps; results for all of them are at the bottom.

Older models: 552 inputs (ppo_state_L1: no loot, in-flight bubbles, enemy kind or air currents) and 616 inputs (everything up to the evolution runs: no projectiles, because the game had none). Agent.load trims the observation so they still run; the game they play now has projectiles they cannot see. Still not in the observation: dead enemies mid-arc (about a quarter second before they become fruit).

Actions

Discrete(9): NOOP, LEFT, RIGHT, JUMP, LEFT+JUMP, RIGHT+JUMP, SHOOT, LEFT+SHOOT, RIGHT+SHOOT. Jump and shoot only fire on a fresh press, so holding the same action across steps does not repeat them.

Two optional knobs, both off by default: time_penalty (stands in for the unimplemented hurry-up) and reward_pops=False, which stops paying the 10 points per bare bubble pop or bounce, since those can be farmed forever.

Multi-level training fails at 2M steps (about 20k per level) and works at 50M. The 2M ppo_state_all agent dies in the first seconds of most episodes; removing the pop reward made it learn almost nothing, so pops are its main early learning signal. The 50M agent also clears level 1 in 48 of 50 episodes, better than the level-1 specialists. Its training return rose until about 16M steps and then hovered around 50. Under training conditions (4-minute limit) it clears 37 of 200 episodes, dies in 92 and runs out the clock in 71, so finishing levels rather than surviving and collecting points is the remaining weakness.

Decaying the learning rate (--anneal-lr) raised the reward but lowered the clear rate: under training conditions the annealed agent averages a return of 61.9 against 55.4, yet clears 18 of 200 episodes against 37 and runs out the clock in 97 against 71. It is better at what the reward pays for, which is staying alive and collecting points for four minutes, not finishing levels. Each configuration was run once, so differences this size are not firm.

Greedy playback (always the most likely action) does worse than sampling on the same model, so sampling stays the default.

What the search found:

  • The winning line kept the baseline reward unchanged. Its only mutation was a lower learning rate (1.5e-4 instead of 2.5e-4), followed by two unmutated continuations. 8 of the final 10 survivors descend from that one generation-1 child.
  • Turning the pop reward off was consistently among the worst choices (39 to 71 of 500), and a clear bonus of 100 on its own also did badly (42).
  • A steep time penalty (0.03) won generation 1 but did not keep up. One line with clear bonus about 32 and time penalty 0.01 survived to the end; it times out least but dies most.
  • So the earlier guess that the reward settings were the bottleneck was not borne out: more training at a lower learning rate, plus selection, gave the gain.

Model architecture

The model is the Agent class in scripts/train_ppo.py: a shared encoder feeding two linear heads (actor-critic). The trained model uses the MLP encoder. About 236,000 parameters in total.

Current model (state observation)

flowchart TB
    In["state observation<br/>656 floats"]
    subgraph Encoder["shared encoder"]
        L1["Linear 656 -> 256<br/>168,192 params"]
        T1["Tanh"]
        L2["Linear 256 -> 256<br/>65,792 params"]
        T2["Tanh"]
        L1 --> T1 --> L2 --> T2
    end
    In --> L1
    T2 --> Actor["Actor head<br/>Linear 256 -> 9<br/>2,313 params"]
    T2 --> Critic["Critic head<br/>Linear 256 -> 1<br/>257 params"]
    Actor --> Dist["Categorical over 9 actions<br/>(sampled, also at playback)"]
    Critic --> V["state value V(s)"]
  • Weights use orthogonal initialisation (gain sqrt(2) in the encoder, 0.01 on the actor, 1.0 on the critic).
  • The actor and critic share the whole encoder; only the last layer differs.
  • There is no memory (no recurrence, no frame stacking). Each decision uses the current observation only.
  • Actions are sampled from the distribution during playback too, so the same model gives different episodes.

How a PPO update flows

flowchart TB
    Roll["Collect rollout<br/>16 envs x 128 steps = 2,048 samples<br/>store obs, action, log-prob, reward, done, value"]
    GAE["Compute advantages (GAE, gamma 0.99, lambda 0.95)<br/>returns = advantages + values"]
    MB["Shuffle, split into 4 minibatches of 512"]
    Loss["loss = clipped policy loss<br/>+ 0.5 x value MSE<br/>- 0.01 x entropy"]
    Step["Adam step, lr 2.5e-4<br/>gradient norm clipped to 0.5"]
    Save["save model.pt, log to TensorBoard"]
    Roll --> GAE --> MB --> Loss --> Step
    Step -- "4 epochs over the same rollout" --> MB
    Step --> Save
    Save -- "until 2M steps" --> Roll

Running your own experiments

Everything here is one command each, runs unattended, and leaves its results under runs/. A full "round" is: train one or more agents, score them on the same fresh seeds, profile what they do, and watch the interesting ones.

Setup, once

uv sync --extra play --extra train

Two habits that matter when several runs share the machine:

  • export OMP_NUM_THREADS=1 before launching. Each run then uses one core; without it every run grabs all 12 and they crawl.
  • caffeinate -is in front of the first run keeps the Mac awake (lid open, plugged in). Everything else can go on in the background with &.

1. Train

export OMP_NUM_THREADS=1
caffeinate -is uv run python scripts/train_ppo.py \
    --levels 1-100 --time-penalty 0.01 --death-penalty 5 \
    --total-steps 100000000 --seed 1 \
    --run-name my_first_run --quiet \
    > runs/my_first_run.log 2>&1 &

Speed is about 14,000 agent steps per second per run, so 100M steps is roughly two hours. The model lands at runs/my_first_run/model.pt (saved continuously, so you can score a run before it finishes), and the log prints the average return of the last 20 episodes once per million steps:

tail -3 runs/my_first_run.log

Curves: MLflow (and TensorBoard)

Every run logs to a local MLflow store (mlflow.db in the repo, SQLite, nothing leaves the machine) and to TensorBoard files in its run directory. To browse runs, compare them and look at the curves:

uv run mlflow ui --backend-store-uri sqlite:///mlflow.db

then open http://127.0.0.1:5000. Each run records all its flags as parameters, the final model.pt as an artifact, and these metrics every 5 PPO updates (--log-every changes that):

MetricWhat to look for
charts_episodic_return, charts_episodic_lengththe reward the agent is actually collecting; length tells you whether it dies fast or runs out the clock
losses_policy_lossPPO's surrogate objective; small and noisy is normal, it is not meant to go to zero
losses_value_losshow well the critic predicts return; should fall, then track the reward scale
losses_entropyhow random the policy is; falling means it is committing to actions, collapsing to ~0 means it stopped exploring
losses_approx_kl, losses_clipfrachow far each update moves the policy; KL above ~0.02 or clipfrac above ~0.3 means the learning rate is too high
losses_explained_variance1 = the critic explains the returns perfectly, 0 = no better than a constant, negative = worse
charts_learning_rate, charts_level_pool, charts_spsthe schedule (--anneal-lr, --curriculum) and speed

TensorBoard shows the same curves: uv run tensorboard --logdir runs/. --no-mlflow / --no-tensorboard turn either off; evolve.py turns both off for its children, since 30 runs per generation is noise in the UI.

The knobs

Reward shaping (what the agent is paid for):

FlagDefaultWhat it does
--time-penalty0charge per step; 0.01 is what produced the bubble-climbing
--death-penalty1charge on death; use 5 with per-step penalties (1 makes suicide optimal, 15 makes the agent timid)
--clear-bonus10reward for clearing a level
--stationary-penalty0charge per step while x stays inside --stationary-band px for --stationary-window steps (15 steps = 1 s)
--no-pop-rewardoffstop paying for popping empty bubbles (hurt badly when tried)

The task:

FlagDefaultWhat it does
--levels 1-100level 1 onlypool of levels, one drawn per episode; "1,5,20-30" works
--curriculum 0.6offgrow the pool from level 1 to all of them over the first 60% of training
--random-startoffplayer starts on a random platform
`--obs stateegoscreen`statewhat the agent sees: absolute positions, player-relative positions, or pixels (pixels are untested)
`--arch mlpconv`mlpflat network, or a small CNN over the tile grid (7x slower on CPU)

Continuing from an existing agent:

FlagWhat it does
--init-model runs/<run>/model.ptstart from those weights (an older, shorter observation is padded with zero weights)

PPO itself (--lr, --ent-coef, --clip, --gamma, --num-envs, ...) rarely needs touching; --lr 1.5e-4 and lower helped slightly in the evolution runs.

2. Score, on the same seeds every time

uv run python scripts/evaluate.py runs/my_first_run/model.pt --episodes 10 --seed 4242

Every level, 10 episodes, one life, one-minute cap: "cleared N/1000". Seed 4242 is the set every number in docs/ uses, so your result is directly comparable. Reference points in the current game (with projectiles):

AgentCleared / 1000
random playabout 10

Noise: re-scoring the same model on different seeds moves it by about 15-20. Differences smaller than that are not real. Add --obs ego for ego models.

3. See what it actually does

uv run python scripts/analyze.py runs/my_first_run/model.pt --episodes 3 --sections behaviour,outcomes

The behaviour profile is the quickest read: horizontal distance and x range per episode (a camper has an x range under 10 px), share of time still, bubble bounces per minute (3 for the old camper, 27 for the climber), and how many episodes cross the screen. --sections can also include ablation (which inputs it relies on), levels (clear rate by level features) and cap (does more time help). The default runs them all and takes about 20 minutes.

4. Watch it

uv run python scripts/export_levels.py runs/my_first_run/model.pt --out exports/my_first_run
open exports/my_first_run/index.html

A GIF per level (best of 3 tries) in a page with green/red/grey bars for cleared/died/timed out. About 2 minutes and 30 MB. For a single level at full speed:

uv run python scripts/train_ppo.py --play runs/my_first_run/model.pt --level 7

writes agent.gif. And to play the game yourself: uv run bubblebobblerl-play --level 7.

Running a round of several at once

Four runs fit comfortably on the 8 performance cores. Put the shared flags in an array (a plain string will not be split by zsh):

export OMP_NUM_THREADS=1
P=(--levels 1-100 --time-penalty 0.01 --death-penalty 5 --quiet)
caffeinate -is uv run python scripts/train_ppo.py "${P[@]}" --stationary-penalty 0.02 --total-steps 100000000 --seed 1 --run-name r1_still02  > runs/r1_still02.log  2>&1 &
uv run python scripts/train_ppo.py "${P[@]}" --stationary-penalty 0.05 --total-steps 100000000 --seed 2 --run-name r1_still05  > runs/r1_still05.log  2>&1 &
uv run python scripts/train_ppo.py "${P[@]}" --init-model runs/v6_band32_from_bouncer_50M/model.pt --total-steps 50000000 --seed 3 --run-name r1_bouncer_more > runs/r1_bouncer_more.log 2>&1 &

Then, when they are done, score them all with one loop:

for r in r1_still02 r1_still05 r1_bouncer_more; do
  uv run python scripts/evaluate.py runs/$r/model.pt --episodes 10 --seed 4242 &
done; wait

Do not edit scripts/ or src/ while runs are going: a run that imports a module late (the evolution workers do) can pick up a half-changed codebase.

Letting evolution do the rounds

scripts/evolve.py automates "train a population, keep the best, reseed":

caffeinate -is uv run python scripts/evolve.py \
    --base runs/v6_band32_from_bouncer_50M/model.pt --name myevo \
    --generations 3 --population 30 --survivors 10 --steps 10000000 --workers 8

Each generation mutates twelve settings (reward shaping and PPO hyperparameters), fine-tunes 30 children in parallel, scores everyone on the same seeds, and keeps the top 10. About an hour per generation; runs/evolve/myevo/leaderboard.md has the ranked table and re-running the same command resumes after a crash. The genome it mutates and the hand-picked first-generation variants are at the top of the script if you want to change what it searches over.

Every child becomes an MLflow run in experiment evolve-myevo (curves, genome, evaluation, survived tag) and each generation adds a summary run. While it runs, the server it starts serves http://127.0.0.1:5001; afterwards the usual mlflow ui command shows the same runs. One lesson from evo10: fine-tuning from a trained agent wants a learning rate of about 5e-5 to 1e-4, not the 2.5e-4 default, or the first few million steps undo it.

Ideas that have not been tried

  • A stationary penalty of 0.02-0.05 with death penalty 5: forces movement, probably at the cost of the climbing and the clear rate. Worth seeing.
  • Paying for distance covered instead of charging for standing still (a few lines in BubbleBobbleEnv.step, next to stationary_penalty).
  • A pool of 10 levels instead of 100, to find out whether a moving agent can be learned at all on fewer layouts.
  • Longer runs: nothing here went past 200M steps.
  • Recording your own play in play.py and pretraining from it (the most likely route to a moving agent that survives, in my view).
